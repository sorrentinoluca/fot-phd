### **1. Introduction** 

As the process of industrial informatization deepens, the massive data streams generated by sensors present unprecedented challenges to traditional machine learning tasks. In response, a variety of methods for analyzing and mining data streams have emerged. However, a notable challenge in online environments is Concept Drift (CD), defined as the evolution of the statistical properties of data over time in dynamic settings. The occurrence of CD can degrade model performance, as the patterns learned during training may no longer apply to new data [1–4]. In industrial settings, CD can result from various factors, including the optimization and adjustment of industrial processes, wear and aging of machinery and equipment, changes in environmental conditions, and new policy implementations. These factors significantly impact models deployed in industrial applications [5]. As illustrated in Fig. 1, effectively detecting and addressing CD not only maintains optimal model performance but also significantly mitigates its potential adverse effects. 

Currently, the field of CD detection has become a focal point among 

researchers, with detection strategies primarily divided into three categories: methods based on statistical testing, model performance monitoring, and data distribution analysis. Statistical test-based methods identify CD by analyzing the statistical properties of data streams. For example, Page et al. [6] proposed the Page-Hinkley test, which relies on the cumulative difference between the observed value and the current mean value as the detection metric. Methods based on model performance monitoring focus on tracking changes in model performance over time to detect CD. Among these, the Drift Detection Method (DDM) [7] is widely used, issuing drifts or warning signals when the overall error rate of the model significantly increases beyond a specific threshold. Other similar methods include the Early Drift Detection Method (EDDM) [8], EWMA for Concept Drift Detection (ECDD) [9], 

and the Hoeffding Inequality-based Drift Detection Method (HDDM) [10]. Finally, data distribution-based methods analyze the differences between historical and new data windows, quantifying the dissimilarities using metrics such as distance. For instance, the ADWIN algorithm

However, despite the extensive range of detection strategies explored in existing research, most focus on classification problems, leaving the detection of CD in regression problems relatively underexplored. 

In classification problems, the method based on the CP framework is preferred for its flexibility in calculating non-conformity scores, and it serves as an effective tool for detecting CD in regression tasks. This approach was initially proposed by Vovk et al. [14], with its core based on CP and Conformal Martingale (CM) to perform exchangeability testing. Furthermore, Ho et al. [15] introduced a universal martingale framework employing exchangeability tests to monitor changes in data streams, thereby demonstrating the effectiveness of this approach in practical applications. In the absence of CD, the CM values should behave as a bounded 

stochastic process; conversely, if CD occurs, the CM values will significantly deviate from the established bounds, indicating a change in model performance. Additionally, Eliades et al. [16] introduced the Inductive Conformal Martingales (ICM) framework for classification tasks, further extending the method’s applications. Compared with traditional methods, the CM framework-based approach makes no assumptions about data distribution, adapts to a wide range of real-world application scenarios, and its sensitivity to new data and real-time capabilities ensure its effectiveness in dynamic and online environments. 

Another significant challenge associated with large data streams is the prohibitive cost of data labeling, which restricts the direct application of traditional supervised learning models. Although supervised detection methods excel in accuracy, they depend heavily on real-time and precise data labeling, which is challenging to achieve in many practical scenarios. In contrast, unsupervised detection methods are constrained by their high false alarm rates [17]. Given the high cost of data labeling, semi-supervised learning provides an effective solution. This approach reduces reliance on labeled data by allowing the model to utilize unlabeled data. Among the semi-supervised learning methods, the self-training strategy is an effective way to address the issue of high data labeling costs. As illustrated in Fig. 2, the initial phase involves using a small set of labeled data to train the model, which is subsequently used to predict the labels of unlabeled data to generate pseudo-labels. Moreover, high-reliability pseudo-labeled samples are selected for inclusion in the training set for retraining the model [18]. In classification tasks, selecting high-reliability samples is relatively intuitive by assessing the output probability values. However, selecting high-reliability samples for regression tasks becomes more challenging due to the lack of clearly defined criteria. At this juncture, the CP framework can provide variable-width prediction intervals for different test samples by setting the form of non-conformity scores calculation. By selecting samples with narrower prediction intervals to join the high-reliability sample set at a specified confidence level, the CP framework offers an effective method for selecting high-reliability samples in regression tasks. This approach eliminates additional computational burdens, allowing this study to utilize a unified and efficient framework.

### _3.1. CP-based semi-supervised learning method_ 

This paper introduces an effective method for identifying highreliability samples within the CP framework. CP quantifies the uncertainty of a sample by providing prediction intervals at specified confidence levels. Specifically, the narrower interval at a given confidence level indicates reduced uncertainty in the model’s predictive ability. Consequently, this paper focuses on selecting samples with the narrowest intervals to create a high-reliability sample set. ### _3.2. CM-based CD detection method_ 

CM is a statistical technique that integrates CP with martingale theory. The martingale is a mathematical tool used for modeling a fair game process, where the expected value of the next observation is equal to the current observation given the past information. CM is constructed from a sequence of values generated by CP. ### _4.3. Analysis of experimental results for CD detection based on the CM framework_ 

Within the CD detection process utilizing the CM framework, a data point is identified as a CD occurrence whenever the CM value surpasses a predetermined threshold. This study conducts a sensitivity analysis on the thresholds for CD detection across various datasets, as depicted in Fig. 8. To circumvent the establishment of excessively low thresholds, which could precipitate the superfluous detection of drift points and thereby squander computational resources, the study considers thresholds ranging from 1.1 to 1.5. The evaluation metrics include the number of detected CD points and the MSE following model updates. Regarding the UCI dataset, as illustrated in Fig. 8(a), a threshold of 1.1 is deemed optimal due to its substantial reduction in MSE after model upgrades. For the alumina evaporation process dataset, depicted in Fig. 8(b), 

although the number of detected drift points remains constant across all thresholds, the threshold of 1.1 is selected as the most favorable, correlating with the minimal updated MSE. In the case of the blast furnace ironmaking dataset, shown in Fig. 8(c), a threshold of 1.15 is determined to be optimal, corresponding to the lowest updated MSE. 

The CD detection results, as illustrated in Fig. 9 and Fig. 10(a), four significant drift points are identified in the UCI dataset, one in the alumina evaporation process dataset, and five in the blast furnace ironmaking dataset. The presence of these drift points signifies a notable change in data distribution, necessitating model adjustments to preserve prediction accuracy. During the experiments, the model undergoes retraining using new data following the detection of drift points. 

Table 2 showcases the test set error performance both before and after the detection of CD. Specifically, in the UCI dataset, the MAE decreases from 3.1068 to 0.8880, making a reduction of approximately 71.42 %, and the MSE dropsfrom 16.3225 to 1.5985, a reduction of roughly 90.21 %; in the alumina evaporation process dataset, the MAE falls sharply from 8.0763 to 3.6705, a substantial reduction of approximately 54.55 %, and the MSE plummets from 89.2823 to 30.0896, a decrease of roughly 66.30 %; in the blast furnace ironmaking dataset, the MAE decreases from 0.0381 to 0.0151, reflecting a decrease of about 60.37 %, and MSE decreases from 0.0048 to 0.0011, showing a reduction of approximately 77.08 %. Fig. 10(b) illustrates the MSE trend for the test set before and after CD detection, clearly showing that the prediction error of the model increases significantly at the first drift point, while the performance continues to improve after retraining. Fig. 11 further demonstrates the significant reduction in the overall MAE and MSE across each test set following CD detection and model retraining. ### **5. Conclusion** 

In this study, a novel semi-supervised CD detection methodology based on the CM framework is introduced, addressing the CD phenomenon that is widespread in a numerous industrial data streams. The approach is specifically designed to confront the dual challenges of high cost of data labeling and effective CD detection. The proposed method significantly enhances the quality of pseudo-labeling and drastically diminishes the model prediction error through a precise CD detection and retraining mechanism. The efficacy of the approach has been demonstrated through multiple regression tasks in various real-world industrial contexts. Moreover, the adaptability and utility of the proposed framework are not confined to regression tasks; they also expand to encompass other data analysis tasks such as classification and clustering. Overall, the research presents a robust and flexible tool for analyzing dynamically changing industrial data streams, ensuring the stability and precision of the model in environments subject to frequent fluctuations.
